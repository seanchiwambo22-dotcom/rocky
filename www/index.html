One rock, three names

The name changes with where the rock is, not with what it is.

Meteoroid

Out in space

A chunk of rock or metal drifting around the Sun. Most are dust-sized crumbs shed by comets and asteroids. Scientists usually cap the size at about a metre; anything bigger is called an asteroid.

Meteor

In the sky

The streak of light, not the rock. A meteoroid slams into air at tens of kilometres per second, and the air in front gets compressed and superheated, glowing and stripping off material. Extra-bright ones are fireballs.

Meteorite

On the ground

Whatever survives and lands. Most meteoroids burn up completely, so a meteorite is the lucky leftover, and a free sample of the early solar system you can hold.

Quick rule: meteoroid is the rock in space, meteor is the light show, meteorite is the rock you find afterward.

Where do they come from?

Almost all of them start out in orbit around the Sun, not far from home.

Asteroids

Most meteorites

Leftover building blocks of the planets, mostly in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Collisions and Jupiter’s gravity nudge fragments onto paths that cross Earth’s.

Comets

Most shooting-star showers

Icy bodies shed dust as the Sun warms them. Earth crosses these dusty trails every year. The grains burn up, so showers rarely leave rocks.

The Moon and Mars

Rare and special

Big impacts can throw rocks off the Moon or Mars. A small number have landed on Earth and been identified.

Rocky’s home address: most likely the asteroid belt, somewhere between Mars and Jupiter.

Falls with a story

Chelyabinsk, Russia, 2013

A rock roughly the size of a building exploded in the air above the city. No big crater: the shockwave arrived minutes after the flash and blew out windows across town. It is the best-recorded airburst ever, thanks to dashboard cameras.

Sylacauga, Alabama, 1954

A stone crashed through a roof and bruised a woman napping on her couch. She remains one of the very few people documented as struck by a meteorite.

Peekskill, New York, 1992

A meteorite landed on a parked Chevy Malibu. The car's owner later sold the car, famously, for far more than it cost.

Hoba, Namibia

The largest known intact meteorite, a slab of iron that is still lying where it fell thousands of years ago. It is too heavy to move, so visitors come to it.

Myths, politely busted

Shooting stars are stars falling

They are not stars at all. A typical shooting star is a grain of dust lighting up for a second or two, tens of kilometres overhead. Real stars are far too big and far too far away to fall.

Meteorites land red-hot

Usually not. The fiery part happens high up. Near the end the rock slows dramatically and the cold of the upper atmosphere has had time to chill its interior, so many finds are cool, or at most warm, to the touch.

Every meteorite makes a crater

Only the biggest, fastest impacts do. Small falls slow down enough to land as ordinary rocks, sometimes making just a small pit in soil.

If it is heavy and weird, it is a meteorite

Most suspicious rocks are earthly (the community nickname is meteorwrongs). Slag, magnetite and plain old basalt fool people all the time. See the field checks below.

Look up: meteor showers

Showers happen when Earth ploughs through the dusty trail left by a comet or asteroid.

Perseids

Peak in mid-August

Debris from comet Swift-Tuttle. Warm summer nights, a crowd favourite.

Orionids

Peak in late October

Debris from Halley's Comet, which means you can watch pieces of it burn up.

Leonids

Peak in mid-November

From comet Tempel-Tuttle, famous for rare, wild storms in some years.

Geminids

Peak in mid-December

Unusual: its source is an asteroid, 3200 Phaethon, and it is often the year's richest show.

Found a rock? Field checks

These do not prove anything, but they are the usual first filters. Labs confirm the rest.

Heavier than it looks

Metal-rich meteorites feel surprisingly dense for their size.

Magnet test

Most meteorites hold some metal and attract a magnet. Many earth rocks do not.

Thin dark crust

Fresh falls have a thin, glassy fusion crust from the burn.

No bubbles

Holes and gas bubbles usually mean slag or volcanic rock.

Think you found one? Photograph it where it lies, note the exact spot, and keep it uncleaned and uncut. Then contact a museum or university: they can test it properly.
